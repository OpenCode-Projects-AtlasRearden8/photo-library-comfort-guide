# Retiring an Old Phone Safely

There is a drawer in your house where phones go to think about their lives. This chapter is how they leave it: photos rescued first, the phone wiped so cleanly it forgets who you are, the battery handled safely, and the send-off either useful, gifted, or properly recycled. An hour, start to finish, mostly waiting-on-device time.

## The order of operations

The sequence matters, because the riskiest steps must come after the important data is safe:

1. [Rescue the photos](#rescue-the-photos-first)
2. Verify the rescue
3. Sign out of every account (yes, before wiping)
4. Remove SIM and SD cards
5. Factory reset
6. Then — and only then — repurpose, gift, trade in, or recycle

Do not skip step five, even for a trusted cousin: saved accounts, app data, message histories, and passwords all ride along on a used phone, and a wipe is the respect step. Doing everything in order also means you never have to un-retire a phone to fetch one lonely photo.

## Rescue the photos first

An old phone is a museum with no gift shop, and it may hold photos that never made it anywhere else:

- **The cable way (most private, fastest).** Plug into your computer and import. Both major phone ecosystems have a built-in import flow; you do not need new software.
- **The drive way.** Copy to a computer folder or an external drive you control, following the [two-copies rule](glossary.md#two-copies-rule).
- **The cloud way.** Let cloud sync ingest the old phone, then verify that the photos are visible on your *current* device before you touch anything else.

**Verify before wipe.** Count the photo roll on the old phone, then count on the destination. Compare counts, or at minimum spot-check the oldest and the newest photos plus one album. This five-minute ritual is the entire difference between relief and a nameless dread. If the old phone's roll contains near-duplicates of photos you already have, that is fine — [Duplicates, Decoded](02-duplicates-decoded.md) sorts that out on *your* schedule, with the phone still alive.

## Sign out of everything

A factory reset on a phone that is still signed in can leave the device lock up in ways that stop the next owner (or even you, at resale) from using it at all. So, battery now at 50 percent or more:

1. Sign out of every account you can find: your primary account, secondary accounts, work and school profiles, and any app store account.
2. Turn off activations and find-my-device features.
3. Make a list mentally of anything with a saved card: your wallet, your transit app, your [Practical and Records](03-albums-youll-actually-keep.md)-adjacent shopping apps. Cards can usually be removed from your account settings from *your* main phone later; note the ones that need it.
4. Unpair or disconnect any accessories: smartwatch, earbuds, car pairings.

## Remove SIM and SD cards

Pop the SIM out and keep it with the phone's paperwork, or cut it up when the line is cancelled; the card holds almost nothing these days, but the drawer holds it better than the trash does. If the phone has an SD card, take the card out and read it on a computer once — it may hold photos from an older era of the phone. Rescue what is there, then wipe the card separately or label it and slide it into the archive box. And while the card is in the reader, glance for any [pocket shots](glossary.md#pocket-shot): the last photos of a phone tend to be the ones nobody ever saw.

## Factory reset

Both major platforms keep the switch in settings under a *reset* or *erase* menu; search the settings for "erase" and take the option that says **erase all content and settings**, not any partial reset. When it finishes, the phone presents itself as a blank new device, in whatever language it woke up with. That blankness is the goal.

## What to do with the empty shell

A wiped phone still has a life. In rough order of usefulness:

- **Transfer it down the family line.** A wiped phone with a decent battery and a case is a fine extra device for a younger sibling, a grandchild, a grandparent, or the kitchen recipe station. Set it up with their account, not yours.
- **Donate it.** Charities take phones; wipe-and-donate is standard. Include the charger when you can.
- **Trade it in.** Retailers and carriers run trade-in programs; wiped phones are the whole point, and the credit is real. Check two offers rather than two hundred.
- **Recycle it properly.** Every city has electronics recycling; many big-box stores and carriers accept drop-offs at the door. Batteries never go in household trash. If the battery is puffy, do not press on it and do not ship it — tell the recycler, and they will handle it.

Nothing here cares whether the phone is five months old or nine years. What matters is that it leaves you complete: photos out, accounts gone, storage blank, and a destination chosen. A rescued, wiped, and handed-off phone has finished its assignment — which is the friendliest thing an object can do.

## The send-off

The last step is optional, and it is human: put the wiped phone in the drawer for one week — the same drawer where phones go to think about their lives — then move it along to its destination with the charger if you have it. Every photo it ever took is either on your roll or in your drive, sorted and dated; the next owner starts from zero, on purpose.

The drawer is now empty. It was never really storage; it was the waiting room. Your roll survives, your archive holds, and the next phone starts its career in a house that knows exactly where photographs belong.
